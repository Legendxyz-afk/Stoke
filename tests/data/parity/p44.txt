The lighthouse had stood at the edge of the cliff for nearly a hundred and fifty years, and in all that time, its keepers had kept a written log of every ship that passed within sight of its beam. Mira had inherited the job from her grandfather two winters ago, along with his heavy iron key ring, his collection of hand-repaired charts, and his habit of talking to the sea as though it could hear him. She had thought, at first, that the work would be lonely in a way she could not bear, but she had come instead to treasure the long stretches of quiet, the rhythm of climbing the spiral stairs each evening to light the lamp, and the particular way fog rolled in over the water just before a storm.

Tonight the fog was thicker than she had ever seen it, a solid gray wall that swallowed the horizon entirely and muffled even the usual cries of the gulls nesting in the rocks below. She had climbed the tower an hour early, uneasy without being able to say exactly why, and stood now at the lamp room window watching the beam sweep out into nothing and vanish. Somewhere out past the reef, faint and irregular, she thought she heard the clang of a bell that did not belong to any buoy she knew of, a sound that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere within the fog at once.

She checked the log her grandfather had kept for decades, running her finger down the columns of dates and ship names until she found, tucked between two ordinary entries from thirty years earlier, a single line written in a hand shakier than his usual careful print: heard the bell again tonight, same as before, will not write more until certain.

Mira told herself, as she had told herself every night since taking over the light, that sound traveled strangely over water in weather like this, bending and doubling back in ways that could turn an ordinary buoy bell into something that seemed to circle the tower itself. It was a reasonable enough explanation, one her grandfather might even have offered himself on any other night, and yet she found she could not quite make herself believe it as she stood there listening to the fog press against the glass.
