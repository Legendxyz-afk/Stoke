The road climbed steadily out of the valley, winding past terraced fields and small stone farmhouses whose chimneys sent thin trails of smoke into the cold morning air. By the time the sun had fully cleared the eastern ridge, the last signs of cultivated land had given way to open slopes covered in short grass and scattered boulders, and the air had grown noticeably thinner and sharper with every switchback. Our guide, a wiry man who had walked this route more times than he could count, set a pace that seemed unhurried but never once slowed, and within two hours the village behind us had shrunk to a handful of pale roofs scattered across the valley floor far below.

We stopped to rest at a small stone shelter perched on a shoulder of the mountain, where a spring trickled out from beneath a slab of gray rock and collected in a shallow pool before continuing downhill. From there, the trail grew steeper and rockier, threading between outcrops where lichen clung in patches of orange and pale green, and twice we crossed narrow wooden bridges spanning gullies carved by centuries of snowmelt. Clouds began gathering around the higher peaks in the early afternoon, and our guide quickened his pace slightly, explaining that the weather here could turn from clear to stormy within the space of an hour.

We reached the high pass just as a thin mist began drifting across the ridgeline, softening the outlines of the peaks beyond into pale gray silhouettes. Prayer flags strung between two weathered posts snapped sharply in the wind, their colors faded from years of sun and snow.

By late afternoon the mist had thickened into a steady drizzle, and our guide led us off the main trail toward a cluster of stone huts used by shepherds during the summer months, where we would spend the night before continuing the descent at first light. Inside, a small iron stove filled the single room with a dry, smoky warmth, and over a simple meal of lentils and flatbread, our guide told stories of travelers who had crossed this same pass a generation before us, back when the trail was marked only by cairns and memory rather than the painted signs we had followed all day.
