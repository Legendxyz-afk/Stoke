Water on Earth is constantly in motion, cycling endlessly between the oceans, the atmosphere, the land, and every living thing, in a process that has continued largely unchanged for billions of years even as the exact paths any single molecule of water takes can vary enormously. The cycle begins, if it can be said to begin anywhere, with evaporation: heat from the sun causes water at the surface of oceans, lakes, and rivers to turn into vapor and rise into the atmosphere, leaving behind any salts or minerals that were dissolved in it. Plants contribute to this upward movement too, through a process called transpiration, in which water absorbed by roots travels up through stems and leaves before escaping as vapor through tiny pores on the leaf surface; together, evaporation and transpiration are often called evapotranspiration.

As water vapor rises, it cools, and cooler air can hold less moisture than warm air, so the vapor begins to condense around tiny particles of dust, salt, or pollen suspended in the atmosphere, forming the droplets that make up clouds. When enough droplets gather and grow large and heavy enough, gravity pulls them back down as precipitation, falling as rain, snow, sleet, or hail depending on the temperature of the air they pass through on the way down. Some of this precipitation falls directly back into the ocean, completing a short loop, while the rest falls on land, where its path becomes considerably more varied and complicated.

Water that falls on land may soak into the soil and become groundwater, flow across the surface into streams and rivers that eventually carry it back to the sea, become locked away in ice and snowpack for months or years at a time, or be taken up again by plants and returned to the atmosphere through transpiration.

Human activity increasingly shapes this ancient system as well: irrigation draws groundwater and surface water onto farmland faster than natural recharge can replace it in many regions, dams reshape the timing and location of river flow, and paving over soil with roads and buildings prevents rainfall from soaking in, sending it rushing into storm drains instead of slowly recharging the aquifers below. Even the composition of the atmosphere itself plays a role, since a warmer world allows the air to hold more moisture, intensifying both evaporation in dry regions and precipitation in wet ones.
