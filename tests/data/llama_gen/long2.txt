The invention of the printing press in the fifteenth century changed the course of human history in ways that its inventor could scarcely have imagined. Before movable type became widespread, every book had to be copied by hand, a slow and expensive process that kept written knowledge in the hands of a small number of scribes, clergy, and wealthy patrons. A single manuscript could take a skilled copyist many months to complete, and even then errors crept in with each new copy, so that the same text might differ subtly from one version to the next. When Johannes Gutenberg combined movable metal type, oil-based ink, and a modified wine press into a single working system, he created a method of reproducing text that was faster, cheaper, and far more consistent than anything that had come before it.

The effects rippled outward quickly. Within decades, printing presses had spread across dozens of European cities, and the number of books in circulation grew from a few thousand to many millions. Ideas that had once traveled slowly, carried by a handful of copied manuscripts, could now reach a much wider audience in a fraction of the time. Religious reformers used the press to distribute pamphlets and translated scripture directly to ordinary readers, bypassing the institutions that had previously controlled access to religious texts. Scientists and scholars could share their findings with colleagues in other countries, compare notes, and build on each other's work far more efficiently than before, which some historians credit as a key ingredient in the rise of modern science.

The press also changed language itself. As printers chose which regional dialects to standardize in their editions, certain forms of spelling and grammar spread and became dominant, gradually smoothing out some of the variation that had existed between neighboring regions. Literacy rates began a slow climb, driven in part by the sudden abundance and falling cost of reading material, and a new class of professional writers, editors, and publishers emerged to meet the growing demand.

By the following century, the printing press had crossed the Atlantic alongside colonists and missionaries, and new presses were being assembled in cities across the Americas to print religious texts, legal notices, and eventually newspapers. Governments quickly recognized both the opportunity and the danger this technology represented, and many moved to license printers, censor unauthorized works, or claim royal monopolies over which books could be produced, showing how thoroughly the invention had reshaped the exercise of political power.

A reef is built slowly, over centuries, by tiny animals called coral polyps, which extract calcium carbonate from seawater and use it to construct hard skeletons around their soft bodies. As generation after generation of polyps grows, dies, and is built upon by the next, these skeletons accumulate into the vast limestone structures that we recognize as reefs, some of which are large enough to be seen from space.

Most reef-building corals depend on a remarkable partnership with microscopic algae called zooxanthellae, which live inside the coral's tissue. The algae photosynthesize using sunlight and share much of the resulting energy with their coral host, while the coral provides the algae with a protected home and a steady supply of the compounds they need to grow. This partnership is also what gives healthy coral its vivid colors, since the algae themselves are pigmented. The relationship is so central to reef survival that when corals are exposed to unusual stress, such as water that is too warm, they often expel their algae in a process known as bleaching, leaving behind a stark white skeleton that will starve and die if normal conditions do not return quickly enough.

Reefs support enormous biodiversity by offering countless nooks, crevices, and surfaces where fish, crustaceans, mollusks, and other organisms can shelter from predators, hunt for food, and reproduce. Beyond their ecological role, reefs also protect coastlines from erosion by absorbing the force of waves before they reach the shore.

Reefs are typically classified into three broad types depending on their shape and relationship to nearby land: fringing reefs grow directly outward from a coastline, barrier reefs form further offshore with a lagoon separating them from the shore, and atolls are ring-shaped reefs that surround a central lagoon where an island has since eroded away or sunk beneath the waves. The largest reef system in the world, stretching along the northeastern coast of Australia, is in fact made up of thousands of individual reefs and hundreds of islands rather than a single continuous structure.

Existing telephone networks were built around circuit switching, where a dedicated line was reserved for the entire duration of a call, an approach that worked well for voice but wasted enormous capacity when used for the short, irregular bursts of data that computers tend to produce. The alternative these researchers pursued was called packet switching, in which messages were broken into small chunks, each labeled with its destination, and sent independently across whatever path happened to be available, only to be reassembled once they arrived.

In 1969, this idea took physical form as ARPANET, connecting four university computers in California and Utah. The first message ever sent between two of these nodes was supposed to be the word login, but the receiving computer crashed after only the first two letters arrived, so in a fittingly modest twist, the actual first message transmitted across what would become the internet was simply lo. Engineers spent the following years refining the protocols that let these scattered networks talk to one another consistently, and by the late 1970s a specification called TCP/IP had emerged as the common language that would allow entirely separate networks to be stitched together into a single interconnected system.

Adoption remained slow and mostly confined to universities, government labs, and a handful of technology companies throughout the 1970s and 1980s, since using these early networks required specialized knowledge and dedicated hardware that ordinary people simply did not have access to.

That changed dramatically in the early 1990s, when a researcher at a physics laboratory in Switzerland proposed a system of linked documents that could be browsed with simple point-and-click software, an idea that was soon paired with graphical web browsers capable of displaying text and images together on ordinary desktop computers. Suddenly the technical barrier that had kept the network confined to specialists began to fall away, and businesses, universities, and eventually private households started connecting in numbers that grew far faster than anyone involved in the original research had anticipated.
